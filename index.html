Fajada Butte Three-Slab Sun Dagger (29SJ297) & Cardinal Cosmology

Rising 135 meters above the southeastern entrance of Chaco Canyon, Fajada Butte (Tsé Diyilí) houses the Three-Slab Site (29SJ297) documented by Anna Sofaer in 1977 (Sofaer et al. 1979 Science 206:283–291). Three leaning slabs of Cliff House Sandstone (~2–3 m tall) channel narrow slivers of sunlight ("daggers") onto two pecked spiral petroglyphs on the cliff face behind them:

  • Summer Solstice Noon (June 21): A single vertical light dagger pierces the exact center of the large 9.5-turn spiral petroglyph.
  • Winter Solstice Noon (Dec 21): Two parallel light daggers bracket the exact outer left and right tangents of the large spiral, leaving its center dark.
  • Spring & Autumnal Equinoxes: One dagger falls midway between the center and right edge of the large spiral, while a second dagger pierces the exact center of the smaller 2.5-turn spiral to the upper left.
  • 18.6-Year Lunar Major & Minor Standstills: Moonrise shadow edges align with the center (minor standstill) and left tangent (major standstill) of the 9.5-turn spiral, with 19 diagonal grooves matching the 18.6-year lunar nodal cycle (Sofaer et al. 1982).

Across the canyon floor below, Pueblo Bonito, Chetro Ketl, and Casa Rinconada encode these same astronomical axes at monumental scale: Pueblo Bonito's central dividing wall and sightline across Chaco Wash to Casa Rinconada follow the true North–South cardinal meridian, while its southern closing wall aligns to the East–West equinox sunrise/sunset, and Room 228 (and adjacent Room 225) in the southeastern corner sector feature exterior corner windows aligned to the Winter Solstice sunrise (~120° ESE; Reyman 1976 Science 193(4257):1037–1038).

TYPE I AD 850–935 (Old Bonito Nucleus)

Uncoursed, unshaped thin tabular sandstone slabs (~3–5 cm thick) laid in abundant buff mud mortar without a rubble core. Preserved in the original 9th–10th century crescent surrounding Room 33 (the High-Status Matrilineal Crypt), Room 32, Room 28, Room 38, and the western Old Bonito block (including Room 326).

TYPE II AD 1020–1060 (Early Classic Expansion)

First true load-bearing core-and-veneer construction (~0.8–0.91 m thick): a rubble-and-mortar interior core faced with dressed sandstone blocks chinked with small tabular spalls to protect the mortar from high-desert rain erosion along the Northern Spine.

TYPE III AD 1060–1085 (Peak Monumental Banded Veneer)

The signature aesthetic of Classic Bonito architecture: alternating horizontal bands of finely pecked rectangular ashlar blocks and multiple tight courses of thin, darker tabular sandstone chink spalls. Used in Great Kiva A, the East/West Wings, and Casa Rinconada.

TYPE IV & McELMO AD 1085–1140 (Late Bonito Phase)

Type IV features uniform courses of thin, tabular dressed stones with minimal chinking; McElmo style uses large, pecked, dimpled rectangular blocks of soft yellowish sandstone laid in double-wythe walls along the South Closing Arc and Pueblo del Arroyo's Hubbard Tri-Wall.
